# Dine Sight — Copy pack

Most of this is spoken, not written. The doorstep script is the most important thing in this kit — rehearse it until it doesn't sound rehearsed.

---

## 1. The doorstep script 🔴 *(your most important asset)*

**Before you walk in:** have their Google listing open on your phone, scrolled to an unanswered review that is at least a week old.

**Opening (to whoever's at the counter):**
> "Namaste — is the owner or manager around? I'll take two minutes, I'm not selling anything you have to pay for today."

**To the owner — lead with their reviews, not your product:**
> "Sir/Ma'am, I was looking at your Google page. You've got [X] reviews and a [4.1] — that's good. But can I show you one thing?"
>
> *(show the phone — an unanswered review, eleven days old)*
>
> "This one's from eleven days ago. There's no reply on it. Which is completely normal — almost nobody replies. But every person who checks your place before coming reads this one, and what they see is that nobody answered."

**Pause. Let them read it.** They will usually say something like "haan, time hi nahi milta."

> "Exactly. That's the whole thing. Nobody has time.
>
> So — can I show you what a reply to this one would look like, in your words?"

*(Open the draft. Read it out loud. **Reading it aloud matters** — it sounds like them, said in their restaurant.)*

> "That's not a template. It knows this review, it knows your place, and it's written the way you'd actually say it.
>
> What it does is: every new review on Google, you get a draft like this within a few minutes. You read it, you tap approve, it posts. Or you change it first. Nothing goes up without you pressing the button."

**The close:**
> "It's free while we're onboarding — I'm doing [neighbourhood] this month. Takes a minute to set up: you sign in with your own Google account, the real Google screen, I never see your password and I can't touch anything except reviews.
>
> Want to just do it now? One minute."

**If they hesitate:**
> "No problem at all. Can I do one thing — give me your WhatsApp and in two days I'll send you a reply we've drafted for your next review. If you like it, you post it. If you don't, delete my number."

**That fallback converts a lot of the maybes.** It costs you nothing and it proves the product with their own data.

## 2. Instagram DM to a restaurant

> Hi! Came across [restaurant] — the [specific dish from their photos] looks great.
>
> Quick thing, not a sales message: I noticed a few of your recent Google reviews don't have replies on them. One from [date] in particular. Most places don't reply, so this isn't a criticism — but people checking you out before booking do read them.
>
> Can I send you what a reply to that one would look like, written in your voice? Takes me two minutes and costs nothing. If you like it, post it yourself.

**Then actually send it.** Do the work before asking for anything. A free, obviously-useful reply drafted for their real review is the most persuasive thing you can do, and it costs you one minute.

## 6. Facebook group post (Restaurant Owners India / city F&B groups)

Framed as a finding, not a pitch.

> **I checked 100 restaurants in [city]. 81 of them had never replied to a single Google review.**
>
> I've been looking at this for a few weeks and the numbers were worse than I expected:
>
> - 81 of 100 had **zero** owner responses
> - Of the 19 who replied, 12 only replied to 5-star reviews *(this is backwards — a reply on a bad review is the one strangers read)*
> - Median time to reply, where it happened: over two weeks
>
> Why it matters: a potential diner reading a one-star complaint with no reply assumes it's true and nobody cared. The same complaint with a warm, specific reply underneath reads completely differently — and the reply is for the **next** reader, not the person who complained.
>
> **Three things that work, if you're going to start:**
> 1. Reply to the bad ones first, not the good ones.
> 2. Name the specific thing they complained about. "We regret the inconvenience" is worse than nothing.
> 3. Do it within a couple of days. After two weeks it reads as damage control.
>
> I've put a free reply generator here if it's useful: [link] — paste a review, get a draft, no signup.
>
> *(Disclosure: I build a tool in this space, so I'm biased. The 81/100 number is real and I'm happy to share the method.)*

**Do the actual survey.** 100 restaurants on Google Maps is two hours and it gives you a post, a statistic you can use in every doorstep conversation, and a prospect list in one go.

## 10. The reply quality standard *(give this away — it's your best content)*

Put this on the free tool page, in Facebook groups, and in every conversation. It proves you understand the job.

> **Good reply to a bad review:**
> *"Thank you for telling us, Priya. Forty minutes is far too long, and cold food isn't how we want your evening to go. We're sorry — please give us another chance, and ask for the manager on the night."*
>
> Names her. Names the specific failure. Apologises once. Offers a concrete next step. Does not argue.
>
> **Bad reply to the same review:**
> *"Dear Guest, We regret the inconvenience caused. Your feedback is valuable to us and has been forwarded to the concerned department. Regards, Management."*
>
> Generic, defensive, corporate. Reads worse than no reply, because now a stranger knows you saw it and said nothing real.
>
> **Never:** argue, blame the customer, mention that they got a discount, or copy-paste the same text under five different reviews. Strangers scroll. They will notice.
