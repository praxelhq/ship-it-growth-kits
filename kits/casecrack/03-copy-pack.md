## 3. LinkedIn — the one long post (week 2 or 3)

> I did ninety-minute case practice sessions for three weeks and got worse at case interviews.
>
> Not literally worse — but my mock scores didn't move, and I'd put in about fifteen hours. The reason took me embarrassingly long to work out: I was doing two sessions a week, and in between I forgot everything I'd learned about my own patterns.
>
> So I switched to fifteen minutes a day. One scoped item, typed or spoken, with follow-ups and a score at the end. Six days a week instead of two.
>
> Three weeks later my scores had moved more than in the previous three months.
>
> **What I think is actually going on:** a ninety-minute case teaches you one case. Fifteen minutes a day teaches you your own failure pattern, because you see it six times a week instead of twice. Mine was deciding the answer in the first ninety seconds and then collecting evidence for it. I needed to be caught doing that about eleven times before I stopped.
>
> That's why the tool I built is built the way it is — short items, and an interviewer that pushes back on the substance rather than just scoring you at the end. The follow-up is the part that catches you.
>
> One thing I was stubborn about: **the follow-ups can't invent facts.** They're anchored to the case's approved data. If it tells you that you missed something, you missed something — it isn't making up a data point to look clever. I'd been burned by that with a general AI tool and it made me distrust every score it gave me.
>
> Five free items, no card: casecrack.live
>
> If you're practising right now, tell me what your pattern is. Mine was premature conclusions. I'd genuinely like to know whether that's universal or just me.

Your best long-term asset. Each is a real page with the full worked reasoning, not a teaser.

Target queries people actually type:

- *how many cars are sold in India every year — guesstimate*

- *market size of gyms in India guesstimate*
- *how many ATMs are there in Mumbai*
- *guesstimate questions with answers for consulting interviews*

**Structure each page:** the question → the structure (supply-side vs demand-side, and why you chose one) → the numbers with stated assumptions → the sanity check → *"the follow-up a real interviewer would ask here"*. Then one line: `Practise this live — casecrack.live`.

**Give away the complete answer.** These rank because they're the best answer on the page, and they still convert because the reader wants the follow-up.

## 9. Replies you'll need all month

**"Is this just ChatGPT?"**

> The model does the language, same as anything in this space would. Three differences: the same rubric every attempt, so your score last week and this week mean the same thing; follow-ups anchored to the case's approved facts so it can't invent a data point you supposedly missed; and a structured debrief you don't have to prompt for. If you're disciplined enough to hold a consistent rubric in your head, you genuinely don't need it.

**"Only five free items?"**

> Five full items, unlimited retries, and scoping questions never count against them. It's enough to know whether the follow-ups are any good — which is the only thing you should be judging it on.

**"What does it cost after that?"**

Answer honestly, and **go publish it** (`04` Fix 2). *"I haven't set a price yet"* is acceptable once in a DM and unacceptable on your pricing page.

**"The library seems small."**

> It is. I'm reviewing items in batches instead of generating hundreds, so breadth isn't there yet. Tell me what type you ran out of and it moves up the queue — that's genuinely how I'm prioritising.

**"Can I speak my answer instead of typing?"**

> Yes — record a voice note and mark it done. Recording and transcription are disclosed and consented before you record. It's the closest thing to saying it out loud to a person, which is how the real round works.
